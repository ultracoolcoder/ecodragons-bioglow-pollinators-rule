Night shift, day shift

When the sun goes down, bats take over. When it comes up, hummingbirds clock in. Together they carry pollen between flowers around the clock, and whole ecosystems depend on them.

Two pollinators, two kinds of flowers

Flowers have shaped themselves to match their visitors. Night bloomers and day bloomers advertise in very different ways.

Bats

  • Active from dusk to dawn
  • Visit big, pale flowers that open at night
  • Find them by strong, musky or fruity scent
  • Long tongues reach deep nectar
  • Pollen sticks to fur, so it travels far

Hummingbirds

  • Active from sunrise to sunset
  • Visit bright red, orange and pink tubes
  • Find them by color, since birds smell little
  • Hover in place and sip with a forked tongue
  • Pollen dusts the bill and forehead

A day in the life

Follow one 24-hour cycle in a desert garden, where agave and cactus bloom at night and trumpet flowers bloom by day. Times are typical, not exact.

7:00 pm, dusk

The bat wakes up

A lesser long-nosed bat leaves its cave roost with hundreds of others. It is hungry after a day of sleep and heads for the desert flowers that are just opening.

9:00 pm, first meal

Pollen on the face

The bat hovers at a pale agave or saguaro flower and pushes its long tongue inside for nectar. Its snout and fur get coated in pollen. At the next flower, some of it rubs off, and that is pollination.

1:00 am, the long commute

Pollen travels for miles

Bats fly many miles between roosts and feeding spots. Pollen that rides along can reach plants far apart, which mixes genes and keeps wild populations healthy.

5:45 am, dawn handoff

Bats head home, birds wake up

The bat returns to its roost as night flowers close. Nearby, a hummingbird warms up from a deep, energy-saving sleep called torpor. It needs fuel fast.

8:00 am, breakfast rush

Sipping at red trumpets

The hummingbird hovers at a red tube flower and licks nectar with its tongue many times a second. Pollen brushes onto its bill and forehead. It feeds again every few minutes, so it visits hundreds of blooms a day.

12:00 pm, midday

Wings blur, pollen spreads

Its wings beat dozens of times a second, which lets it hover, back up and dart from flower to flower. Each stop moves pollen along. Many hummingbird flowers have no landing platform, so the bird does the work.

7:00 pm, shift change

The cycle starts again

The hummingbird fills up one last time and settles in to rest. As light fades, the night flowers open and the bats stream out of the cave. The garden never goes unpollinated.

Why this matters for nature

Pollination is how plants make seeds and fruit. Those seeds and fruits feed birds, mammals, insects and people, and the plants shelter countless other species.

Fruit and seedsSaguaro and agave fruit feed many desert animals after bats pollinate the flowers.
Hundreds of plantsMore than 500 plant species are visited by bats, and hummingbirds serve many more across the Americas.
Healthy genesLong-distance pollen flow links far-apart plant groups, so they adapt better to change.
Food for peopleAgave, which is used for tequila and mezcal, and some fruits like wild bananas rely on bat visits.

How you can help

  • Plant native flowers, including night bloomers for bats and red or orange tubes for hummingbirds.
  • Skip pesticides, which harm pollinators and the insects bats eat.
  • Turn off extra outdoor lights at night so bats can feed and navigate.
  • Keep out of caves and roosts, especially in summer when bats raise pups.
  • Rinse feeders and refill them with 1 part white sugar to 4 parts water. Skip dyes and honey.
