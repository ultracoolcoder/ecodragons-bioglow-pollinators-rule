Our field notes

On a trip to a garden center, we found a whole section of plants labeled NATIVES. A big sign said to plant natives to support pollinators and conserve water. We wanted to know why, so we read every plant tag and looked up more facts.

What is a native plant?

A native plant grew wild in a place long before people started planting gardens there. Texas native plants have lived alongside Texas bees, butterflies, moths, bats and hummingbirds for thousands of years. Their flowers bloom at the right times and have the right shapes for the pollinators that live here.

Four reasons to plant natives

🤝

Old friends

Native plants and native pollinators grew up together. The flowers are the right shape, color and size for the animals that live here.

💧

Water savers

Natives are used to hot Texas summers and dry spells. Once their roots are settled, many need very little extra water.

🍂

Food all season

Different natives bloom at different times. Some, like azure sage, bloom late in the year when lots of other flowers are done.

🐛

Baby food

Some natives are host plants. Butterflies lay eggs on them so their caterpillars have the right leaves to eat.

What surprised us

Not every helpful plant is about flowers. Switchgrass is pollinated by the wind, but skipper butterflies lay their eggs on it and songbirds eat its seeds. Timing matters too. Azure sage blooms late in the year, when many other plants have finished flowering and pollinators still need food.

We also learned that a plant tag is like a tiny instruction book. It tells you how much sun a plant wants, how much water it needs, how big it grows, and how cold it can get before it is in trouble.

Our big idea

If every family planted even one or two native plants, Texas pollinators would have many more places to stop for food. A few pots on a porch count too. Pick plants that bloom at different times, skip the bug spray, and your garden can glow from spring to fall.
